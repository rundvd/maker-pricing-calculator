A laser engraving pricing calculator turns one design into a price that covers the blank, machine use, your labor, and selling fees. Follow this guide to check each cost before you quote a customer or publish a listing.

How to price laser engraving step by step

Enter the blank or sheet cost for one finished item first. A coaster, ornament, and engraved sign can have very different base costs, even if they share a design. Add masking, finish, or other supplies used for that item. If you cut several pieces from one sheet, divide the sheet cost by the number of usable pieces rather than the number you hoped to fit.

Enter run time per item, then the laser price, expected life in hours, and upkeep per hour. Run time should include the machine time needed for the actual job, including repeated passes when the material needs them. The equipment cost spreads the laser purchase across its working life; upkeep covers routine parts and service. If a job needs several passes, update run time instead of relying on a flat price for all designs.

Enter average power draw and your electricity rate. Electricity may be small per piece, but it is still part of the cost. Count the power used by the setup included in your estimate and revise the average when you measure it.

Next, enter hands-on time for artwork checks, focusing, placing the blank, masking, cleaning, finishing, inspection, and packing. Your labor rate applies to those minutes, even when the laser does most of the marking. Add packaging and other per-item costs. Set a failure allowance for scorched blanks, wrong settings, or pieces that fail inspection. Last, select the sales channel, target profit margin, and shipping charged to the buyer. A quoted price should cover selling fees as well as production costs.

Worked example

Use a $1.80 blank or sheet cost and $0.15 for masking and finish. Enter 12 minutes of run time, a $1,200 laser, 6,000 hours of expected life, $0.25 upkeep per hour, and 350 watts of average power draw. Add 5 minutes of hands-on time, $0.60 for packaging and other costs, $25 per hour for labor, $0.18 per kilowatt-hour for electricity, and a 3% failure allowance. Select Etsy, a 40% target profit margin, and $0 shipping charged to the buyer.

Material is $1.80 + $0.15 = $1.95. The calculator displays $0.09 for machine use, $0.01 for electricity, $2.08 for labor, $0.60 for packaging, and $0.14 for failures. Displayed total cost is $4.88. At a $10.99 selling price, displayed Etsy fees are $1.49 and profit is $4.62. The resulting margin is 42%, and profit per machine-hour is $23.09. Line items and totals are rounded separately, so the displayed figures may differ by a cent when added manually.

The target margin is 40%, but the displayed margin can be higher when the calculator rounds the price to a practical selling amount. A larger engraved area, extra pass, or longer cleanup changes the quote. Save separate estimates for products that take different amounts of setup work.

What most sellers forget

  • Masking, stain, finish, and cleaning supplies belong in material cost.
  • A rejected blank costs more than another few minutes of laser time.
  • Artwork cleanup and test burns use hands-on time and sometimes extra stock.
  • A detailed engraving may require more passes than a simple cut.
  • Etsy fees can apply to shipping and gift wrap charged to the buyer.

Profit per machine-hour

This example earns $4.62 over 12 minutes of laser run time, or $23.09 per machine-hour after the calculator's rounding. That measure helps you compare a quick batch item with a long engraved panel. Use it alongside total profit, because a high hourly rate on a single small item may still produce only a few dollars.

FAQ

How much should I charge for laser engraving?

Add the blank, supplies, machine use, electricity, active labor, packaging, failures, and selling fees. Then set a price that meets your margin goal. The example here sells for $10.99 with a $1.80 blank and 12 minute run time.

Should I charge for laser setup time?

Yes, count artwork checks, focusing, masking, placement, and cleanup as hands-on labor. If several identical pieces share one setup, spread that setup time across the batch. Do not charge the full setup to every piece unless you repeat it every time.

How do I price engraved items made from a sheet?

Divide the cost of the sheet by the number of usable finished pieces. Include offcuts or damaged areas in that estimate. Then add each piece's run time, labor, and finishing supplies.

What profit margin should I use for laser engraving?

Choose a target that covers your costs and leaves a return you accept. The 40% target in this example produces a displayed 42% margin after price rounding. Recheck the margin when your blank cost, run time, or sales channel changes.

How the price is calculated

True cost = material + machine wear (purchase price ÷ expected life hours, plus upkeep) + electricity (watts × hours × your rate) + hands-on labor + packaging, then a failure allowance for runs you have to redo.

Suggested price = (true cost + fixed fees) ÷ (1 − percentage fees − target margin), rounded up to the next .49 or .99. That way the margin you asked for is what is left after the marketplace takes its share.

Profit per machine-hour tells you which products deserve your machine time. A cheap item that runs fast can beat an expensive one that ties up the machine all day.
